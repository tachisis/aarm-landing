The cappuccino principle

In business conversations, we try not to say anything to customers that would sound ridiculous if it were said while they were ordering a cappuccino.

“Quite right. Not to worry. We’ll put together a thoughtful proposal outlining our cappuccino approach and aim to revert within a fortnight.”

“…Right. And the price?”

“We should be able to advise on that in roughly two weeks as well, once we’ve gathered the necessary requirements from all relevant stakeholders regarding the definition of ‘cappuccino’.”

There are no domainless projects

If we are doing a project for a client, we always bring in a freelancer who understands the client’s field. We also won’t pretend to “deeply understand customer’s business model before beginning of any project”.

We realise that our job is to develop solid mathematical ideas and turn them into working code in a sensible way. For that we need good mathematicians and programmers. We also need a basic understanding of the area where the code will be used — whether it is transport, cooking efficiency, or something else.

To support this, we always have someone inside available to advise us and guide the work between status calls.

Engineers never timeshare

Goldratt’s Critical Chain Project Management argues that most project bottlenecks appear between stages of work, rather than inside the stages themselves. When people and resources are moved around, there are hidden effects. These effects may not be obvious at the time, but they influence planning, commitments, and delivery.

Because of this, at AAIC each engineer works on only one project. Tech leads, scientists, and designers are different. They can work across projects because their work involves fewer transitions between stages.

It’s only interesting on the frontier

Most of our colleagues are highly experienced in their fields. We have a world champion, several PhDs, former product owners of major global products, former Netflix 3D artists, and others. They are with a weird little service company because we focus on new and unusual problems.

We refuse projects that anyone could do. We do not sell to enterprise clients. A typical senior developer from an outsourcing firm would be considered junior here. The highest-paid people are the most capable thinkers, not the managers.

So, it’s not a boring place to be and not a boring way to live.

They built cathedrals without Scrum

Most products in the world have only one chance to be finished and released. You cannot edit a film once it is in cinemas. You cannot redesign wings once an aircraft is in the air. We try to ship software that, once complete, will not need many changes for years.

This, admittedly, is an idealistic approach and works best in narrow mathematical systems. But even when we build games, we still ask a single question: what if we only had one chance to get it right?
