The Giants

A series of profiles on the giants whose shoulders we stand on. From the earliest days of computing to the present, these are the people who have shaped our world.

ALAN TURING

Logic, Mathematics, Computing, Cryptography

Alan Turing formalised the limits of what machines can compute, gave us the universal machine idea that still describes how computers work, and helped break enemy ciphers during the Second World War. His 1950 paper on machine intelligence asked whether computers could think — and proposed an operational test we still argue about today.

Prosecuted for his sexuality and barred from the work he loved, he died in 1954. His legacy runs through every branch of computer science and every conversation about artificial intelligence.

ADA LOVELACE

Mathematics, Computing

Ada Lovelace collaborated with Charles Babbage on the Analytical Engine and wrote what is often regarded as the first published algorithm intended for a machine. She saw that numbers could stand for symbols, music, and more — not just arithmetic — a leap toward general-purpose computation.

She died young, in 1852, but her notes anticipate ideas that would take a century to build: programs, loops, and the creative partnership between human intent and mechanical execution.

JOHN VON NEUMANN

Physics, Mathematics, Computing, Game theory

John von Neumann moved between quantum foundations, explosion physics, and economics with unsettling ease. For computing, his name is attached to the stored-program architecture: instructions and data live in the same memory, the template for nearly every computer since.

With Oskar Morgenstern, he co-authored the founding text of modern game theory. He died in 1957; the machines he helped design were only beginning to reshape science and society.

CLAUDE SHANNON

Information theory, Mathematics, Engineering

Claude Shannon asked how much information a channel can carry and how to protect it from noise. His 1948 paper created information theory — the mathematical backbone of telecommunications, compression, and much of what we now call data.

At Bell Labs and MIT he also built juggling robots, chess machines, and unicycle robots, treating serious mathematics and playful hardware as one continuum. He died in 2001, as digital networks swallowed the planet he had quietly wired in theory.

GRACE HOPPER

Computing, Programming languages

Grace Hopper helped program the Harvard Mark I, popularised the idea of machine-independent languages, and drove the first compiler, A-0 — a step toward writing software in something closer to human language. COBOL, still discussed in enterprise computing, carries her advocacy for readable, business-oriented code.

A U.S. Navy rear admiral and relentless teacher, she insisted that we waste enormous human time making computers easier for people, not the other way around. She died in 1992.

DONALD KNUTH

Computer science, Mathematics

Donald Knuth set out to write the definitive account of algorithms and instead created The Art of Computer Programming — a living monument that trained generations to care about correctness, analysis, and elegance. His work on TeX reshaped how mathematicians and scientists publish.

Knuth’s insistence on rigour and literate programs is a standing argument against treating software as disposable glue. He remains a reference point for anyone who believes computing is a proper intellectual discipline.

JOHN MCCARTHY

Artificial intelligence, Computer science

John McCarthy coined the term artificial intelligence for the 1956 Dartmouth workshop and invented Lisp, a language whose symbolic structure shaped AI research for decades. His contributions include time-sharing systems and ideas about commonsense knowledge that later projects still chase.

He died in 2011, having watched AI winter and spring repeat, always arguing that intelligence is a problem in engineering and logic, not mysticism.

MARVIN MINSKY

Artificial intelligence, Cognitive science

Marvin Minsky co-founded the MIT AI Laboratory and built some of the first neural-network machines. His book The Society of Mind proposed that intelligence emerges from many simple agents — an idea that echoes in modern modular systems and multi-agent design.

Provocative and encyclopaedic, he pushed researchers to ask how perception, memory, and reasoning might actually be implemented. He died in 2016, leaving a long shadow over both symbolic AI and its critics.

GEOFFREY HINTON

Artificial intelligence, Machine learning

Geoffrey Hinton kept working on neural networks when most of the field had abandoned them, refining backpropagation and architectures that would eventually dominate deep learning. His students and collaborators carried those methods into speech, vision, and language at industrial scale.

He shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics for foundational work on artificial neural networks — a recognition that the ideas once dismissed as fringe now underpin much of modern AI.

YANN LECUN

Artificial intelligence, Machine learning

Yann LeCun developed convolutional neural networks that made practical computer vision possible — the same family of models behind face recognition, medical imaging, and autonomous perception. As a leader at Bell Labs, NYU, and Meta, he bridged academic rigour and large-scale deployment.

He argues forcefully for self-supervised learning and architectures that reason about the world, not just predict the next token. He shared the 2018 Turing Award with Hinton and Bengio.

YOSHUA BENGIO

Artificial intelligence, Machine learning

Yoshua Bengio helped establish deep learning as a field through work on representation learning, attention, and generative models long before they were fashionable. At Mila in Montreal, he built one of the world’s largest academic collaborations on AI.

In recent years he has focused on the societal risks of powerful systems, advocating for safety research and governance without pretending the technical questions are separate from the political ones. He shared the 2018 Turing Award with Hinton and LeCun.
