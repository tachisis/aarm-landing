The Giants

In 1675,

Newton wrote a letter to Robert Hooke containing the well-known line:

“If I have seen further it is by standing on the shoulders of giants.”

Here, then, are the giants upon whose shoulders we stand for this purpose — with Newton, somewhat ironically, not included among them.

Alan Kay

  • Wikipedia
  • Doing with Images Makes Symbols (1987)
  • The Computer Revolution Hasn't Happened Yet (1997)

In 1975, a twelve-year-old girl sat down at a terminal at Xerox PARC and wrote, in an afternoon, a working animation program in Smalltalk.

Alan Kay tells this story not as a boast but as evidence — evidence that the computer, properly designed, could be as natural and as generative a medium as drawing or writing.

Kay had built Smalltalk for exactly this purpose: a programming language organised around objects that send messages to each other, clean enough for a child to reason about, expressive enough to build almost anything.

The interface paradigm that emerged from his group's work at PARC — windows, icons, menus, the mouse — went on to become the visual language of every desktop and smartphone on the planet.

Kay has been pointing out ever since that this is only a fraction of what was intended.

The Dynabook, his 1968 vision of a portable personal computer for children — networked, dynamic, used for genuine intellectual exploration — remains, in his view, unrealised.

He is not wrong.

Bret Victor

  • Wikipedia
  • worrydream.com
  • Dynamicland
  • Inventing on Principle (2012)
  • The Future of Programming (2013)

In a 2012 talk at a student software conference in Montreal, Bret Victor sat down at a laptop and, while writing a simple game in real time, moved a single slider — and the entire physics of the game shifted on screen, instantly, without compiling, without waiting, without breaking the thread of thought.

The audience laughed, not because it was funny but because they recognised, somewhere in their bodies, that this was how it should have always worked.

Victor's argument — that creators need an immediate connection to what they're making, that a delay of even a few seconds between decision and consequence is enough to quietly lobotomise the creative process — sounds obvious once stated, and that's precisely how you know it's true.

He had worked at Apple helping design the earliest concepts for the iPad, but eventually left to pursue the idea to its logical conclusion.

Today he runs Dynamicland, a non-profit research lab in Oakland where the computer is not a screen on a desk but the entire room: projectors and cameras turn physical surfaces into live computational environments, and people work together with their hands, with paper, with real objects.

The system is called Realtalk.

It is, at the moment, the most interesting thing being built in computing, and almost nobody has heard of it.

Doug McIlroy

  • Wikipedia

Somewhere in the early 1970s, a Bell Labs engineer named Doug McIlroy looked at the growing mass of Unix utilities being written around him and had a thought simple enough to fit on a napkin: programs should do one thing and do it well, and they should work together.

The UNIX pipe was his invention, and it is one of the most generative ideas in the history of software.

It is a philosophy made executable: keep things small, keep them composable, trust the user to assemble the pieces.

McIlroy also invented macros, contributed foundational work on sorting, and spent decades at Bell Labs producing work of quiet, lasting consequence.

His periodic critiques of software bloat — of programs that accumulate features until they do everything badly — read today less like complaints than like elegies for a discipline that briefly knew what it was for.

Frank Rosenblatt

  • Wikipedia

In 1957, at the Cornell Aeronautical Laboratory, Frank Rosenblatt built a machine out of motors and potentiometers and wires that could learn to tell the difference between cards marked on the left and cards marked on the right — not because it had been programmed with the rule, but because it had adjusted, through experience, the weights on its connections.

He called it the Perceptron.

Rosenblatt was a psychologist, and that matters: he was not trying to build a useful tool, he was trying to understand how biological brains acquire knowledge, drawing directly on Donald Hebb's earlier theory that neurons which fire together wire together.

The machine was a working hypothesis about cognition.

The 1969 book by Minsky and Papert that demonstrated its mathematical limitations effectively ended the field for a decade, and Rosenblatt did not live to see it revived — he died in a sailing accident in 1971, aged forty-three.

The deep learning systems that now underpin most of modern artificial intelligence are, in their essential architecture, descendants of the thing he built in that laboratory.

Ivan Sutherland

  • Wikipedia
  • Sketchpad thesis (PDF)
  • Sketchpad Demo

Ivan Sutherland's 1963 MIT doctoral thesis ran to a hundred and sixty pages and described, in meticulous detail, a program called Sketchpad — a system that let a user draw directly on a screen with a light pen, define geometric constraints between shapes, and manipulate the results in real time.

This was the first interactive graphical computer program in history, and it introduced, almost in passing, the concept of objects with properties, of master figures and instances, of constraints that a system solves rather than a user specifies.

Alan Kay, who has thought about this longer than almost anyone, has called it one of the greatest programs ever written.

Sutherland went on to win the Turing Award, to found the research group that became the engine of computer graphics, and to contribute to work that now ships in every chip-design tool on the market.

Christopher Alexander

  • Wikipedia
  • A Pattern Language
  • The Timeless Way of Building

In the mid-1970s, the architect Christopher Alexander and his colleagues spent several years walking through cities and towns, asking a question that sounds almost embarrassingly soft: why do some places feel alive, and others don't?

The answer he arrived at, after enormous amounts of empirical observation, was structural.

Alive places, he found, are built from a recognisable set of recurring patterns — a window seat, a sheltered outdoor space, a pedestrian street at a certain scale — each of which resolves a genuine tension between competing human needs.

He called this "the quality without a name," which is either evasive or precise depending on how you read it; he meant that the thing is real and verifiable even though our vocabulary hasn't caught up with it.

His 1977 book A Pattern Language catalogued 253 of these patterns, from the scale of regions down to the thickness of a wall.

Kent Beck and Ward Cunningham read it and recognised immediately that the same logic applied to code.

By 1994, Erich Gamma, Richard Helm, Ralph Johnson and John Vlissides had distilled that recognition into Design Patterns, the book that gave software engineering a shared vocabulary it has never relinquished.

Alexander was reportedly ambivalent about this adoption.

He felt the patterns in that book were too mechanical, too concerned with structure and not enough with the experience of the person inside it.

Saul Kripke

  • Wikipedia
  • Naming and Necessity

In 1959, a paper appeared in the Journal of Symbolic Logic that resolved a problem logicians had been circling for decades: how to give modal logic — the formal study of necessity and possibility, of what must be true and what could have been otherwise — a rigorous mathematical semantics.

The author was Saul Kripke.

He was eighteen years old and still in high school.

The framework he introduced, in which statements are evaluated across collections of "possible worlds" connected by accessibility relations, gave philosophers and logicians a precise and flexible tool for reasoning about counterfactuals, knowledge, obligation, and time.

Then, in 1970, he gave three lectures at Princeton — published a decade later as Naming and Necessity — that demolished a fifty-year philosophical consensus about how names work.

Names, he argued, are not disguised descriptions.

"Water" doesn't mean "the clear liquid we drink"; it refers, rigidly, to whatever stuff actually has the molecular structure H₂O, in every possible world where that stuff exists.

The implications rippled through philosophy of language, metaphysics, and the theory of mind.

What we find most valuable in Kripke is not just the conclusions but the method: he took a set of intuitions that everyone shared but nobody had quite organised, found the hidden structure underneath them, and left the whole landscape cleaner and more honest than he found it.

Genrikh Altshuller

  • Wikipedia
  • The Innovation Algorithm

In 1950, Genrikh Altshuller, a young Soviet engineer and inventor, wrote a letter to Joseph Stalin explaining that the Soviet Union's approach to innovation was systematically wrong and needed to change.

He was arrested shortly afterwards and spent several years in a labour camp in Siberia, where he continued to think about the problem.

What Altshuller had been working on, before and after the gulag, was a study of hundreds of thousands of patents from across industries and decades, looking for the underlying logic of genuine invention.

The system he extracted is called TRIZ — the Theory of Inventive Problem Solving — and its central claim is this: the most creative solutions are not compromises between competing requirements, but dissolutions of them.

Where an ordinary engineer sees a trade-off — the structure must be strong but also light, the process must be fast but also accurate — an inventive engineer recognises a contradiction, and a contradiction is not a dead end but a destination.

The best creative problem is one that contains an internal conflict, because resolving it without compromise is what invention actually is.

Altshuller spent the rest of his life systematising this insight into a teachable methodology.

Gamma, Helm, Johnson & Vlissides — the Gang of Four

  • Wikipedia
  • Design Patterns

In 1993, a group of software engineers met in a mountain cabin in Colorado, at the invitation of Kent Beck and Grady Booch, to work on a problem that had been nagging at the field: good programmers kept solving the same problems in the same ways, and there was no shared language for what they were doing.

The following year, Erich Gamma, Richard Helm, Ralph Johnson and John Vlissides published Design Patterns: Elements of Reusable Object-Oriented Software, which named twenty-three of these recurring solutions — Factory, Observer, Decorator, Strategy, Visitor and the rest — and described, for each, the problem it addresses, the structure it takes, and the trade-offs it involves.

The book was directly inspired by Christopher Alexander's A Pattern Language, and it did for software what Alexander had done for architecture: it made visible a set of structures that experienced practitioners already used intuitively but had never collectively named.

The names turned out to be the point.

Once you could say "that's an Observer pattern," you could discuss it, critique it, teach it, and choose deliberately not to use it.

Johan Huizinga

  • Wikipedia
  • Homo Ludens

In 1938, with Europe moving rapidly toward war, the Dutch cultural historian Johan Huizinga published Homo Ludens — "Man the Player" — a book arguing that play is not a secondary feature of human life but one of its generative roots.

Law, ritual, poetry, philosophy, even warfare, he contended, carry within them the structural characteristics of play: they are bounded in time and space, governed by rules that participants accept freely, separate from ordinary life, and accompanied by a particular quality of absorption and tension that he found impossible to reduce to anything simpler.

His definition has held up remarkably well: play is a free activity, proceeding within its own limits of time and space, according to fixed rules, and absorbing the player entirely.

What makes it useful — genuinely useful, not just elegant — is that it captures both the seriousness of play and its separateness.

Play is not trivial; it is simply not for real, which is precisely what makes it a space in which real things can happen.

Every designer who has tried to think carefully about what a game actually is has ended up, sooner or later, at Huizinga.

Lev Vygotsky

  • Wikipedia
  • Mind in Society
  • Thought and Language

Lev Vygotsky died of tuberculosis in 1934, aged thirty-seven, having spent most of his working life in conditions of material scarcity and institutional hostility, and having produced, in roughly a decade of serious work, a body of ideas that developmental psychology spent the rest of the twentieth century unpacking.

His most enduring contribution is the concept of the Zone of Proximal Development: the gap between what a learner can do alone and what they can do with skilled guidance.

The zone is not a deficit to be closed; it is where learning actually happens, the productive edge between mastery and the not-yet-possible.

Design anything for learning — a curriculum, a game, a tool, a conversation — and Vygotsky's question is the right one to ask: are you working in the zone, or are you either boring the learner with what they already know, or losing them in what they cannot yet reach?

He also argued, with force and evidence, that thought is not prior to language but shaped by it, that cognition is always social before it is individual, and that the tools a culture provides — including its words — are not neutral containers but active shapers of what can be thought.

The reason his work stays with you is that it treats the learner, at any age, as someone in the middle of something — not lacking, but reaching.
